# MTB Patent Atlas

**45 years of mountain bike invention — who patented it, who fought over it, and when it became free for everyone to use.**

An interactive timeline of mountain bike intellectual property: 274 patents and applications spanning 1869 to 2026, organized into nine categories, with dedicated views for the industry's major litigation, a live stats dashboard, four distinct visual themes, and zero build tooling required. A single HTML file that opens in any browser.

## What's in it

### Nine categories
Rear Suspension · Forks & Damping · Drivetrain · Wheels & Tires · Components · E-MTB & Electronics · Frame & Standards · Bike Transport · Cameras & Wearables

### 📈 Patents-per-era bar chart
A scrubber above the filter bar renders the full dataset as a tappable bar chart, bracketed by year. Three bracket sizes are available via the toggle in the top-right of the scrubber:

- **2yr** (default) — the balance point between too dense to read and too coarse to be useful
- **1yr** — every year gets its own bar
- **5yr** — half-decade buckets (e.g. 1995–1999, 2000–2004 are each a single bar), for a zoomed-out view of eras

Tapping any bar jumps straight to that era's section in the timeline. Bars carry a tooltip with the exact count; a subset get a visible year label underneath, thinned automatically on narrow screens so labels never collide. Where the dataset has a real multi-decade silence (e.g. between the earliest 1890 entry and the next one in the mid-1970s), a dashed gap marker breaks the axis rather than drawing the two eras as if they were adjacent.

### ⚔ Patent Fights view
A dedicated tab frames the industry's eight major disputes as named rivalries — not just a filtered list, but head-to-head case cards showing the combatants, what the fight was actually about, how it ended, and tappable chips that jump directly to the affected patent cards in the timeline:

1. **Weagle v. Trek** — the concentric-pivot war (Split Pivot vs. ABP)
2. **Fox v. SRAM** — bleed-valve and damper fight, transferred to Colorado District Court
3. **SRAM v. the Industry** — narrow-wide chainring claims against Race Face, Fox, Praxis, Wolf Tooth
4. **Stan's v. Specialized** — Bead Socket Technology tubeless rim patent
5. **Speedplay v. Bebop** — founder-license pedal case, Federal Circuit (2000)
6. **Spot v. Gates** — belt-drive Drop-Out dropout royalty suit, currently active in Denver District Court
7. **Knolly v. Intense** — seat-tube geometry patent, resolved May 2021 on prior art
8. **RampWerks v. absoluteBLACK** — ramped/oval chainring patents, voluntarily dismissed without prejudice June 2026

### 📊 Stats view
A dedicated tab that turns the raw dataset into a live dashboard: total/active/expired/litigated counts, a status-split bar, entries-by-category, a **patents-by-category-over-time chart** (below), and a most-patented-names leaderboard (brands and inventors combined, top 12). Every bar is tappable — clicking a category or a name jumps straight into the filtered timeline. Nothing here is hand-maintained; it's all computed from `D` at render time, so it can't drift out of sync the way a written-out stats table can. **The leaderboard counts atlas entries, not real-world patent holdings** — its own intro text says so, because for a handful of names (GoPro, SRAM, Shimano, Fox) the gap is large: this atlas deliberately catalogs a curated, MTB-relevant subset rather than a company's entire portfolio (see *Bundled entries*, below), so a low bar next to a famous name means "lightly represented here," not "holds few patents."

### 📈 Patents by category, over time
A stacked bar chart in the Stats tab, bucketed into fixed 5-year brackets from 1980 onward, independent of the top-of-page scrubber's 1/2/5-year toggle (the two charts serve different questions — the top chart is "how much activity in this era," this one is "what kind"). A **Count / Share** toggle switches between two readings of the same data: Count (the default) scales each bar's height to that bracket's total filing volume, so a busy era's bar is visibly taller; Share holds every bar at full height and shows only the category mix, so you can compare composition across eras — e.g. rear-suspension's early dominance giving way to newer categories — without volume differences drowning out the comparison. Either way, each colored segment is one of the eight core categories plus a ninth, "Cameras & Wearables," sized to its share; hovering a segment shows its exact count and era in a tooltip, and a legend below the chart maps every color to its category name so identity never depends on guessing a hue. The eight core category colors are a fixed, validated categorical palette (checked for colorblind-safe adjacent-pair separation, not picked by eye); "Cameras & Wearables" deliberately does *not* get a competing 9th hue — the palette method this atlas follows caps at 8 fixed hues by design, so the newest, thinnest category renders as a neutral gray "Other"-style segment instead of stretching the palette. Segments are separated by a thin gap in the surface color rather than a border, so touching categories stay visually distinct without adding ink that isn't data. Entries filed before 1980 (16 as of this writing — scattered 19th/early-20th-century bicycle-mechanics patents with decades of silence between them) aren't dropped from the chart; they're folded into one leading "**\<1980**" bar, so the regular grid stays dense and readable without a real entry disappearing from the total.

### ⏳ Going free soon
A dedicated Status filter surfaces active patents estimated to expire within the next two years — the atlas's forward-looking answer to its own tagline about technology "becoming free for everyone to use." Qualifying cards carry an amber "⏳ free by [year]" pill. The same list appears at the bottom of the Stats tab (below Most-patented names) as a tap-to-jump watchlist.

### 🔗 URL reflects the current view — and two ways to share it
Every filter combination, search term, active tab, and individual card has its own URL — the browser's address bar updates automatically as you filter and browse, and reloading or bookmarking a page returns to the same view. Individual cards resolve by patent number (or a title slug for unverified entries), so a URL can point at one specific patent. Two things put that mechanism one click away: a **🔗 Share** button in the filter toolbar (next to Surprise Me) opens a small dropdown — Copy link, or post straight to X, Facebook, or Reddit, each pre-filled with the *current* URL including whatever filters/search/open card are active — and every card also carries its own small send-icon **copy-link button** beside the expand/collapse toggle, which copies a direct link to that one entry regardless of what's currently filtered. "Copy link" and the per-card buttons share one clipboard helper that falls back through `navigator.clipboard` → a hidden-textarea `execCommand("copy")` → a manual-copy `prompt()`, since the Clipboard API is frequently blocked inside the cross-origin iframe this tool is designed to run in. The three platform options are plain share-intent link-outs (`https://x.com/intent/...` and equivalents) opened in a small popup — no SDK, no Web Share API, no added dependency. The toolbar menu is a native `<details>` (same zero-JS-to-open pattern as the "All N patents on Google Patents" disclosure on multi-patent cards) and closes itself after a click, on Escape, or on an outside click.

### 🎲 Surprise me
Jumps to a random card from whatever's currently visible (respects active filters), opens it, and scrolls it into view. A low-effort way to surface entries from deep in a 243-item catalogue that most visitors would otherwise never scroll to. The button also gives itself a subtle shimmer at a random interval (roughly every 20–90 seconds) as a passive hint that it's interactive — it never interrupts a click and turns itself off for anyone with reduced-motion preferences set.

### 🌐 Language switcher (English / French)
An EN/FR toggle sits in the header next to the theme switcher. Every piece of UI chrome — filter labels, tabs, buttons, tooltips, the Stats and Patent Fights view copy, the footer, and the Colorado MTB Innovation panel — is fully translated. **The French text for the patent write-ups themselves (titles, summaries, and "why it matters" analysis) is AI-drafted and has not yet been checked by a French speaker against the English original** — a banner says so on-page for as long as that's true, and stays until a real review happens. This is a deliberate, disclosed tradeoff, not an oversight: patent-law and suspension-engineering terminology is exactly the kind of text where a wrong word is worse than no word, so it's flagged rather than presented as finished. All 273 entries have a drafted French version as of the latest pass — the 7 that briefly rendered English-only under French (added to `main` while the initial translation branch was in flight: the three absoluteBLACK EU design entries, the two WickWerks/RampWerks chainring patents, the RampWerks v. absoluteBLACK litigation entry, and the Shimano Ice Tech rotor) were caught up in a follow-up pass rather than left as a permanent gap. It combines with the other deep-link params too (`#cat=susp&lang=fr`), using `lang=fr`/`lang=en` instead of the bare form in that case.

Eight tabs across the top of the filter bar: Category · Status · Story · Inventors · Brands · All · 📊 Stats · ⚔ Fights. The Inventors and Brands rows (the long ones) have left/right scroll arrows that appear only when content overflows, soft fade-edge cues, and a "Show all" button that wraps everything onto multiple lines for full at-a-glance access.

### International patent office links
Entries with non-US filings carry a jurisdiction tag and link out to the relevant national patent office (DPMA, UK IPO, INPI, PRV, EPO/Espacenet, IP Australia, CIPO, UPRP) or to the direct Google Patents record when available. Taiwan's 3-letter TWI prefix and US reissue patents (RE-prefix) are handled automatically by the link-building logic. `j:"EU"` is a deliberately separate jurisdiction from `j:"EP"`: EPO/Espacenet covers European *patents*, while EUIPO (linked for `EU`) administers Registered Community Designs — an industrial-design registration, not a utility patent. EUIPO designs aren't indexed by Google Patents at all, so `EU` entries always carry `num:null` and cite their RCD numbers (format `NNNNNNNNN-NNNN`) in prose instead of a per-record link.

## Embedding in Pinkbike or similar platforms

The page works as a standalone link or an `[IFRAME]` embed. Pinkbike's embed syntax:

```
[IFRAME width=text height=1100 mobileHeight=1800 src="https://postmillennium-mtb.github.io/MTB-PATENT-ATLAS/"]
```

**Known limitation:** `position: sticky` (filter bar) and `position: fixed` (Colorado panel) resolve against the *iframe's* own viewport, not the host page. Inside a fixed-height iframe, the sticky filter bar scrolls away when the reader scrolls the article page and doesn't return without manually scrolling inside the iframe. On mobile this is the most noticeable friction point. A `?embed=1` mode that wraps the atlas in its own internal scroll container would fix this cleanly and is a planned improvement. Test in an actual iframe before publishing.

**Deep links inside an iframe:** the visible browser address bar always reflects wherever this document is actually loaded from — the standalone `github.io` URL, or the iframe's own address when embedded — never the parent page that embeds it. That's a consequence of how the hash-routing is written (this document can only rewrite its own `location` via `history.replaceState`, not a cross-origin parent's), and it's the right behavior for that mechanism specifically: it's the only URL that resolves correctly on its own without any cooperation from whatever page is embedding it. If the browser blocks URL rewriting inside a sandboxed iframe (some embed contexts do), the tool degrades gracefully: filtering and browsing still work, only the "address bar reflects current view" behavior is skipped.

**Share, copy-link, and cite-this-entry links are a separate mechanism** from the address-bar behavior above, and deliberately don't follow it: every "📋 Copy link," per-card share button, and "cite this entry" link is built against a fixed `SHARE_BASE_URL` constant (`https://postmillenniumrenaissance.com/atlas/`) rather than this document's own address, so what a reader actually copies or shares is always the on-brand postmillenniumrenaissance.com URL — never the raw `github.io` implementation detail — regardless of whether the page happens to be running standalone or embedded when they click it. This works because pmr-website's own `/atlas/index.html` already reads its own `location.hash` on load and appends it straight onto this tool's iframe `src`, so a link like `https://postmillenniumrenaissance.com/atlas/#p=maestro-suspension` opens that page, which loads the atlas into its iframe with that same hash, which this tool's own existing deep-link logic then reads exactly as it would from a bare `github.io` link. That forwarding only runs once, on that page's own initial load, so it resolves a fresh share link correctly but does not live-sync the parent page's own address bar while someone browses around inside an already-open embed — the address-bar paragraph above still applies to that case.

**These links carry the current language, too.** All three deep-link builders (`citeUrl`, the per-card share button, and `syncHash()`, which the general Share menu and the browser's own address bar both read from) append `&lang=fr` whenever `state.lang` isn't the English default — mirroring `langFromHash()`, which has been able to *read* a `#...&lang=fr` combination since the language switcher shipped, but had nothing writing one until this pass. Before this fix, sharing, citing, or copying a link while reading in French silently handed the recipient an English page — the copied citation *text* was already correctly French (`tx()` covers that), only the URL embedded in or alongside it wasn't. English is still the unmarked default (a plain `#p=slug` link, still the overwhelmingly common case, doesn't grow a redundant `&lang=en`).

## Data schema

All content lives in the `D` array near the top of the `<script>` block. Each entry is a plain JavaScript object:

```js
{
  y: 1996,                    // year filed (required)
  g: 1997,                    // year granted, or null if pending
  t: "Title (US 1,234,567)",  // put the headline patent number in the title when there is one
  a: "Assignee / inventor",   // free text, shown on the card
  num: "1234567",             // primary patent number — plain digits, no commas, no prefix
  nums: ["1234567","7654321"],// OPTIONAL: only when a real portfolio needs multiple links
  cat: "susp",                // susp | fork | drive | wheel | comp | emtb | frame | transport
  st: "active",               // active | expired | pending | unknown
  exp: 2017,                  // estimated expiry (filing year + 20), or null
  j: "DE",                    // OPTIONAL: omit entirely for US filings
  b: ["litigated"],           // licensor | acquired | givenaway | litigated | priorart
  who: ["Trek"],              // brand/inventor filter tags — must exist in BRANDS or INVENTORS
  conf: "v",                  // v = verified | m = unverified number | l = draft / in progress
  s: "Factual summary...",    // 1–3 sentences, what the patent covers and its history
  w: "Why it matters..."      // editorial payoff — what changed because of this patent
}
```

`exp` also drives the **Going free soon** filter and Stats watchlist automatically — active entries with `exp` within two years of the current year surface there with no extra tagging needed.

A single entry can also stand in for a whole product family rather than one specific filing — several GoPro entries do this deliberately (see *Bundled entries*, below) rather than adding a card per patent in a 1,600+ patent portfolio.

### Patent number formatting

Store numbers as plain digit strings with no commas, spaces, or `US` prefix. The `numLink` / `patentUrl` functions detect prefixes and format display labels automatically:

| Type | Store as | Displays as | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard US grant | `"7334846"` | US 7,334,846 | Stan's BST |
| US design patent | `"D896132"` | US D 896,132 | — |
| US reissue patent | `"RE45684"` | US RE 45,684 | Manitou |
| 2-letter country prefix | `"EP3111109"` | EP 3,111,109 | MRP Wave Ring |
| 3-letter country prefix | `"TWI386342"` | TWI 386,342 | Manitou Taiwan |
| WO publication | `"WO2025003104"` | WO 2025/003,104 | KOM Xeno Hub |
| US application | `"20190136918"` | US 20190136918 | Bill Shook ratchet ring |

### Confidence tiers

The `conf` field is an honesty mechanism, not cosmetic metadata:

- **`"v"`** — verified: patent number independently confirmed against a primary source (USPTO, Google Patents, a manufacturer's own virtual-marking page, or court filings)
- **`"m"`** — medium: the technology and story are confirmed but the specific number wasn't independently re-verified this pass
- **`"l"`** — draft: existence confirmed, number not yet located, or research still in progress

Several entries (WRP, EXT, Forbidden, Lauf's original Grit fork, Antidote, OPEN BAR, and the bundled GoPro category entries) are intentionally tagged `"m"` or `"l"` because the underlying companies operate on a deliberate patent-pending or trade-secret strategy, the entry stands in for an entire portfolio rather than one filing — not because of incomplete research. When adding entries, prefer an honest `"m"`/`"l"` over inflating to `"v"`.

### Bundled entries

A handful of entries represent a *category* of patents rather than a single filing — currently GoPro's action-camera portfolio (image processing/FOV, stabilization, hardware/acoustics, telemetry), grouped into four cards instead of one per patent. Use this pattern when a company's IP position matters to the story but individually cataloguing a 1,000+ patent portfolio would swamp the rest of the dataset. Bundled entries cite one representative patent number where a specific filing is genuinely the best example, and are tagged `conf: "l"` since no single number fully verifies the bundle.

### Adding a new brand or inventor

Every value used in an entry's `who[]` array must already exist in `BRANDS` (companies) or `INVENTORS` (named individuals, `{label, key}` pairs) near the top of the script. If a chip doesn't appear in the filter row, the missing registration here is the first place to check.

### Adding a new Patent Fight

Add an object to the `FIGHTS` array with: `key`, `title`, `sub`, `combatants[]`, `era`, `stakes`, `outcome`, and `cards[]`. The `cards[]` array takes patent number strings or distinctive title substrings; `fightCardMatches()` scans all `D[]` entries and auto-generates tap-to-jump chips for every match.

### A note on card identity

Each rendered card gets an HTML `id` of `p-<patent number>` (or a slugified title if there's no number), used by both the deep-link and surprise-me features to jump to a specific entry. Adding a new patent doesn't require touching this — the id is generated automatically from existing fields at render time.

## Methodology and sourcing

Compiled from USPTO and Google Patents records, manufacturer virtual-marking pages (Fox, Wolf Tooth, Hayes Bicycle Group, MRP, Shimano, Manitou, and others), court filings, and industry reporting (Pinkbike, BikeRadar, Bicycle Retailer, Singletracks, MTBR, Singletrack World, Vital MTB, Escape Collective).

Primary-source verification is preferred throughout. Where verification wasn't possible, entries carry an honest confidence label rather than inflated certainty. This includes checking third-party product claims against the primary patent record rather than taking them at face value — a product marketed under a "chin mount" name, for instance, doesn't necessarily mean the seller holds a patent on the mechanism, or that a patent with a similar-sounding title is actually the same invention.

Expiration dates are estimated as filing year + 20 years and do not account for terminal disclaimers, maintenance fee lapses, or patent term adjustments. **Nothing here is legal advice.**

For patents that issued before June 8, 1995 (when US law switched to a 20-years-from-filing term), `exp` is instead estimated as **grant year + 17 years** for utility patents or **grant year + 14 years** for design patents, matching the actual pre-GATT statutory term. The atlas's three Schwinn entries (1938–1966) are the only current cases old enough for this to matter, but it's the correct rule for any pre-1995 filing added later.

Corrections, additional patents, and better sourcing are welcome — open an issue or PR.

## A note on the "Tech" category question (resolved 2026-09-12)

This was an open question from July 2026 through mid-September: every entry then fit the existing eight categories (`susp fork drive wheel comp emtb frame transport`), including the GoPro entries, split across `comp` (image processing, stabilization, hardware/acoustics) and `emtb` (telemetry). The tradeoffs as they stood at the time:

**For a new category:** GoPro doesn't cleanly fit "Components" (a physical bike part) or "E-MTB & Electronics" (motor/battery systems specifically) — it's neither. Future camera-and-sensor entries would have a clearer home, and the Stats view's category breakdown would more accurately reflect where the dataset's weight sits as this area grows.

**Against, at the time:** only 5 entries fell in this space (4 GoPro bundles + the chin mount) — thin enough that a dedicated filter tab risked reading as a single-brand category. A ninth category was also a real identity change: the README, meta description, and site tagline all described "eight categories."

**The decision, once made:** add `tech` ("Cameras & Wearables") as a ninth category. By the time this was acted on, two more entries (Forcite smart-helmet electronics, multi-camera wireless sync) had joined the space, growing it from 5 to 7 — past the point where folding it into an existing category was honest categorization rather than a shrug. The scope test applied when migrating: does the invention control/modify how the *bike itself* functions (shifting, damping, dropper actuation, motor drive, braking)? If yes, it stayed in its functional category regardless of being electronic — Di2, AXS, Flight Attendant, Bosch drive units, and every other electronically-actuated bike system already in the atlas were checked against this and **none moved**. Only entries that are rider-worn camera/data-capture accessories genuinely orthogonal to the bike's own systems qualified: all 7 GoPro-family entries (image processing/FOV, stabilization, hardware/acoustics, Forcite electronics, multi-camera sync, GPS telemetry, and the chin mount).

**Implementation, as it played out:** category rendering *was* fully data-driven from `CATS` as this note predicted — the filter chip, card pills, and "Entries by category" Stats bar all picked up the new category with zero code changes beyond the registry entry itself, and the filter-tab row's existing horizontal-scroll behavior (already relied on for the longer Inventors/Brands rows) absorbed the 9th chip without any mobile-crowding fix needed. The one piece this note didn't anticipate: the Stats tab's "Patents by category, over time" chart, added after this note was first written, color-codes category identity — and that palette method is structurally capped at 8 hues (see `CAT_COLORS`'s own comment in `index.html`). `tech` renders there as a neutral gray "Other"-style segment instead of a 9th competing hue, which is that method's own prescribed handling for exactly this situation, not a workaround.
